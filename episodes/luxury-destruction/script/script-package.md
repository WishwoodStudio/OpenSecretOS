## Final Script

> Burberry burned £28.6 million of its own clothes.
>
> Not a one-off write-down. Every year, same line: "finished goods
> physically destroyed." Ninety million pounds, over five years.
>
> And Burberry wasn't the only one. Cartier's parent company, Richemont,
> spent 481 million euros buying back its own unsold watches — then took
> them apart, piece by piece.
>
> Two different companies. Same decision: zero dollars, on purpose,
> instead of a discount. Why?
>
> Because a marked-down coat in an outlet bin isn't one lost sale. It's a
> public price tag — visible to every full-price customer — for the
> entire collection.
>
> So it's never "destroy it" versus "sell it for something." It's
> "destroy it" versus "quietly discount everything else, too." Count it
> that way, and zero is the cheaper number. This is the markdown tax.
>
> Burberry got caught and stopped, in 2018. France banned the practice
> outright, in 2022. You can regulate the method. You can't regulate the
> math.
>
> So next time a luxury brand offers you a "members-only" resale
> channel, or keeps a restock deliberately tiny — there's a different way
> to read it.
>
> It's not exclusivity built for you. It's the markdown tax, paid in a
> different currency — your attention, your patience, the appeal of
> being let in.
>
> So the next time you see a brand protecting its scarcity — a waitlist,
> a limited drop, an "invite-only" anything — ask yourself: is that
> exclusivity? Or is it the markdown tax?
