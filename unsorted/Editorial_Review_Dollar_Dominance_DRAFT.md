## Step 1 — What is the viewer actually asking?

The title makes a promise. Here's what it subconsciously commits us to answering, graded honestly:

| Viewer's real question | Expectation | Emotional importance | Does our current mechanism (financing loop) answer it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Is this because America is still the strongest economy?" | High | High | **Indirectly.** We correct it rather than answer it — the whole point is that it's *not* about size. Good, if the correction lands cleanly; risky if it just feels like a dodge. |
| "The title says 'still' — is the dollar's dominance actually fragile / ending?" | High | Very high | **No.** Round 1's own risk section (§8) rules out the de-dollarization/collapse framing as G2-incoherent. This is a real gap: the title promises engagement with fragility, and we've deliberately built an episode that avoids it. |
| "Is this about oil (the petrodollar)?" | High | Medium | **No.** Round 1 explicitly rejected building on oil. This is the single most common wrong belief in Step 1's list and the episode currently never touches it. Leaving it completely unaddressed risks a large slice of the audience feeling their specific misconception went unacknowledged. |
| "Could another currency replace it — the euro, the yuan?" | High | High | **Yes, well.** This is the strongest match in the whole research — Round 2's euro/RMB comparison answers this directly and provably. |
| "What does this mean for me?" (T5, implicit but required) | Medium (unstated) | High (it's a hard gate) | **Not yet.** Neither round has produced a concrete personal-stakes line. This is a genuine gap, not just an omission — Constitution Governance Rule 9 makes this a script-lock gate, not optional. |

**Critical read:** two of the five things the title promises (the "still"/fragility angle, and the petrodollar myth) are currently unaddressed by design, for good reasons (political risk, and factual weakness of oil framing). That's a defensible editorial choice, but it means the *title itself* is over-promising relative to what the episode delivers. The episode we've built answers "why doesn't the obvious explanation (size, power) hold up" and "why can't a rival displace it" — it does not answer "is this about to end" or "is it about oil." Worth flagging now: either the title needs to narrow to match the delivered promise, or the script needs at least a fast, deliberate bust of the petrodollar belief so it doesn't sit as an unaddressed elephant in the room.

## Step 2 — What is the episode really about?

Three honest interpretations of the research, evaluated as editorial stories rather than as economic arguments.

### A. The dollar survives because of network effects
- **Central reveal:** usage causes more usage.
- **Emotional payoff:** low. It restates the premise ("dollar is used a lot because it's used a lot") rather than revealing anything underneath it.
- **Belief transformation:** shallow — already flagged as a tautology in Round 2 Part 4. Doesn't survive a single follow-up question from an engaged viewer.
- **Visual simplicity:** high (a simple loop), but an empty loop — nothing is being explained, just drawn.
- **Risks:** fails the Surprise dimension outright. Not a real candidate; kept here only for completeness.

### B. The dollar survives because leaving is too expensive — nobody wants to go first
- **Central reveal:** dollar dominance isn't a preference, it's a trap. Every country holding dollars would arguably be fine with an alternative in principle — but no individual country can afford to be the first one to move, because moving first (alone) is expensive and everyone else standing still makes it worse. It's a coordination trap, not a vote of confidence.
- **Emotional payoff:** strong. This is a universally recognizable human dynamic — nobody wants to be the first to leave the group chat, the party, the relationship, the game of chicken. It converts an abstract monetary-policy fact into something the viewer has felt in a completely different context. That recognition ("oh, it's *that* dynamic") is the transformation.
- **Belief transformation:** strong and direct. It doesn't just add a fact — it replaces "the dollar wins because it's strong" with "the dollar persists because nobody can win by moving first," which is a genuine Type A correction of a specific wrong causal belief (Editorial Handbook §4 — Type A is appropriate exactly here, since "power causes dominance" is factually wrong, not just incomplete).
- **Visual simplicity:** high. One idea, one diagram — countries/currencies each eyeing the exit, nobody moving. No second case study required to complete the thought.
- **Risks:** needs disciplined framing so "trap" reads as an elegant structural feature (engineered) rather than nations being victims of the dollar (exploited) — a real but manageable tone risk (Constitution §7).

### C. The dollar survives because no competitor will make the political sacrifice required to replace it
- **Central reveal:** the euro and the yuan don't lose to the dollar on merit — each is missing one specific, nameable thing (a unified safe asset; an open capital account), and getting it requires a political sacrifice (fiscal union; loss of capital control) neither is willing to make.
- **Emotional payoff:** strong, but a different flavor — closer to "insider explanation" satisfaction than the visceral "oh, that trap" recognition of B. It answers a question rather than triggering a feeling.
- **Belief transformation:** strong, and it's the best answer to "could this end" — but it requires the viewer to hold two comparisons (EU, China) simultaneously to land, which is real cognitive weight for a short.
- **Visual simplicity:** medium. Needs two case studies side by side, not one clean loop — heavier than B to diagram in the time available.
- **Risks:** the most exposed to G2 (Coherent) failure. Explaining why the EU won't pool fiscal sovereignty and why Beijing won't lift capital controls sits close to country-macro and political commentary — exactly what §11 excludes. Handleable, but it's the riskiest of the three by a clear margin.

### Ranking
**B > C > A.**

B produces the clearest single transformation with the least risk and the least cognitive load. C is genuinely excellent material — but as *proof*, not as the center. A doesn't survive scrutiny as a standalone reveal.

## Step 3 — Does the financing loop belong at the center?

Challenging Round 2's own conclusion directly: no, it doesn't. It's evidence, not the reveal.

"Trading in dollars makes borrowing in dollars cheaper" answers a *how* question — it explains the plumbing that makes the trap in Interpretation B real and permanent rather than just psychological. But a viewer doesn't finish a video with a financing mechanism in their head; they finish with a *feeling* about how the world works. The financing loop is precisely why no country can afford to move first (the mechanism), but "nobody can afford to move first" (the trap) is the thing that's actually surprising, transferable, and memorable. Per the Mechanism Ladder's explicit warning against Depth Bias: a deeper mechanism is not automatically a stronger episode, and the Ladder instructs choosing the level that maximizes transformation, not the level that is most technically complete. The financing loop is Level 3 on the ladder (how the trap is enforced); the trap itself is Level 2 (how it happens) — and Level 2 is the stronger first episode. Demote the financing loop to Proof.

## Step 4 — The emotional center

Five candidates for the one sentence the viewer keeps after watching. No jargon, no tautology.

1. **"Everyone's waiting for someone else to jump first. So nobody jumps."**
2. "The world isn't stuck with the dollar — it's stuck holding it, waiting for someone else to let go first."
3. "Every country holding dollars is waiting for the others to blink first."
4. "Nobody uses the dollar because they trust it. They use it because nobody wants to be the one who moves first."
5. "The dollar doesn't have to be the best currency. It just has to be too expensive to leave alone."

### Ranking
1. **#1** — shortest, most quotable, purely natural language, no reference to money at all yet unmistakably about the topic once the video has run. The strongest "stuck in your head" candidate.
2. #4 — best explicit contrast structure (not trust, but fear of moving first); slightly longer.
3. #2 — clear and direct, good on-screen typography candidate.
4. #3 — solid, "blink first" is vivid, marginally less universal than "jump."
5. #5 — clean but reads slightly more like an economics statement than the other four.

**Recommendation: #1**, as the line the episode is built to leave behind, with #2 or #4 as the spoken reveal-beat variant (more explicit about the dollar) immediately before or after it.

## Step 5 — Reveal architecture

**Prior belief → rising tension:** The viewer opens believing the dollar's dominance is a reflection of American strength — the biggest economy wins. Tension builds not through a single contradiction but through an accumulating asymmetry: America's share of world trade is shrinking, and the dollar's grip is tightening anyway. Each beat should make the "size explains it" belief feel less and less sufficient, without yet naming the real mechanism — the viewer should be actively wondering what else could explain a currency getting *stronger* while its home economy gets *relatively smaller*.

**Reveal → proof → resolution:** The reveal lands the trap: nobody's holding the dollar because they trust it most — they're holding it because no one can afford to be the first to let go, and that's what actually explains the asymmetry. Proof follows in two fast, light-touch beats rather than a full technical explanation: first, the mechanical reason moving first is expensive (trading in dollars makes borrowing in dollars cheaper, so leaving means giving that up alone), and second, the fact that the two currencies with real capacity to lead an exit — the euro and the yuan — are each missing one specific thing they've chosen not to build. Resolution closes on the emotional center line (Step 4, #1), reframing the viewer's mental model from "the dollar is strong" to "the dollar is a trap nobody can be first to leave" — Type A correction, engineered register, no nation named as victim or villain.

---

## Step 6 — Final recommendation

1. **What is this episode fundamentally about?** A coordination trap — the world isn't choosing the dollar, it's stuck holding it, because no single country can afford to be the first to let go.
2. **What should the audience remember one hour later?** "Everyone's waiting for someone else to jump first. So nobody jumps."
3. **Which single mechanism belongs at the center?** The first-mover trap / switching-cost lock (Interpretation B) — not the financing loop, not the euro/RMB comparison.
4. **Which mechanisms should appear only as supporting evidence?** The financing loop (dollar invoicing ↔ cheap dollar financing) as the mechanical proof of *why* leaving is expensive; the euro/RMB comparison, compressed, as proof that no rival is currently willing to pay the price of leading an exit.
5. **Has the editorial center changed after this review? Yes.** Round 2 ended with the financing loop positioned as the reveal itself. This review demotes it to proof and promotes the switching-cost trap — a shallower mechanism on the Ladder, but per the Mechanism Ladder's own governing principle, the level that produces the strongest first transformation, not the deepest one. The euro/RMB material survives fully intact, but moves from "part of the center" to "the proof that makes the trap provable rather than asserted." Also flags two open items for the Reveal Brief stage: the petrodollar myth currently goes unaddressed and may need a fast bust; and T5 (Personal Stake) has no candidate line yet from either research round and needs to be solved before script lock.
