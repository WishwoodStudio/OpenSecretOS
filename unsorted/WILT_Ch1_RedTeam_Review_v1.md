# WILT — Chapter 1 Red-Team Review v1

**Status:** Adversarial validation exercise. This document does NOT modify canon.
**Baseline under challenge:** Chapter 1 Opening — Final Design v1.0 (LOCKED), plus post-lock additions (the Hold, hinge objects, interaction-density targets, first-sixty-seconds artifact).
**Success criterion:** Either produce a Chapter 1 concept demonstrably superior on the five evaluation criteria, or conclude explicitly that the locked design remains the strongest option and state why.
**Rule of engagement:** Challengers must respect game-level canon — the Non-Negotiables, the Hold mechanic, node navigation, the Visual Direction Specification, and the House diorama budget. This is a Chapter 1 challenge, not a WILT redesign. Novelty is not rewarded.

---

## 1. The baseline, stated as a steelman

Before attacking the locked design, it must be stated at full strength. The v1.0 opening is built on four load-bearing decisions:

1. **Denial is the world's resting state.** The player wakes inside the wish. Wished Light is not a mode; it is where the world sits when the player does nothing. Truth costs sustained physical effort (the Hold).

2. **Preference drives corruption.** Returning to Wished after seeing True is not neutral — it advances erasure. The player's comfort-seeking is itself the corrupting act. This is the single deepest mechanic-theme fusion in the design: the opening does not *show* the player complicity, it *manufactures* it out of their real behavior.

3. **Implication at minute three.** The first photo reveal is engineered to land as "what did I do," not "who is missing." The central emotional accusation arrives before any mystery framing can take root.

4. **Aversion, not locks.** The forward pull at the sequence's end is a door the protagonist cannot make himself enter — an internal obstacle, camera pull-and-flinch, no mystery-box grammar.

Production footprint: 2 nodes, ~6 small textures, one new behavioral component (ErasureProgression), one new interaction rule. Complexity: Low. The design is gated by the Beat 5 erasure-attribution playtest (players must attribute the erasure to their own action, not a glitch or haunting).

Any challenger must beat this on: **emotional impact, player curiosity, clarity of the Wished/True relationship, production feasibility, commercial appeal.**

---

## 2. Challenger concepts

Each challenger represents a genuinely different opening *philosophy*, argued at its best before being stress-tested. These are not variations on the baseline; each replaces the baseline's implication engine with a different one.

---

### Challenger 1 — "The Release" (inverted onboarding; complicity through the body)

**Philosophy:** Teach comfort as seduction somatically, before the mind can rationalize it.

**Concept:** The game opens mid-Hold. The protagonist wakes gripping the Wishwood; diegetically, the player's thumb is already on the screen, and the room is True — honest, cold, a single mug, one chair. The first input the game ever asks for is *letting go*. Release floods the world with Wished warmth. The first lesson is relief. Every subsequent Hold is then the player choosing to return to the cold room, and every release is a small, physically pleasurable surrender the player has already learned to want.

**Emotional journey:** Disorientation → relief → creeping suspicion of the relief itself.

**Curiosity engine:** "Why does letting go feel like this? What is the warmth covering?"

**Best case for it:** It weaponizes the Hold's asymmetry from second zero and makes the player's body an accomplice before a single narrative object is read. No challenger uses the mechanic more aggressively.

**Stress test:**
- **Clarity of Wished/True — damaged, not improved.** Opening in True anchors it as "the real one" and Wished as a comfort mode the player activates. This collapses the ambiguity that Non-Negotiable #4 (both realities deserve equal dignity; the player should feel genuinely torn) depends on, and it undermines the Visual Direction's non-answer test (neither state is named the correct one). The baseline's Wished-first opening keeps default-reality ambiguous; this challenger resolves it in the first frame.
- **Recognition grammar broken.** The Visual Direction lock establishes that True disturbs through *recognition* — which requires the Wished baseline to be seen first. True-before-Wished has nothing to recognize against; it reads as generic cold-apartment liminality.
- **Genre miscoding.** A cold, desaturated first frame is horror/liminal store language — precisely the misreading the atmospheric-mystery repositioning and the v1.0 legibility pass were engineered to prevent.
- **Commercial.** The first-sixty-seconds artifact is designed around a warm hook that turns wrong. An App Store preview that opens cold loses the "beautiful, then wrong" conversion arc.
- **Onboarding risk.** Teaching "you are already holding" before teaching the Hold is a tutorial inversion with real confusion risk on touch, and it complicates the erasure-attribution gate, which needs clean Wished-first causality.

**Verdict on Challenger 1:** Higher ceiling on somatic impact; loses on clarity, commercial appeal, and canon coherence. Rejected.

---

### Challenger 2 — "The Guest" (her-present opening; the external hook)

**Philosophy:** Open with the thing that was wished for. Maximize hook intensity.

**Concept:** The Wished morning includes *her* — within canon presentation limits: a silhouette at the stove, stillness at the window, back turned, humming the shared song. The strongest possible first frame in store terms: the game opens with the wish fulfilled. The first Hold reveals not an empty room but something quieter and worse — the room is arranged as if no one has ever stood there. No vanishing-person shock; an absence of *arrangement*. Floor unworn where she stands. One mug. Curiosity engine: "Is she real?"

**Emotional journey:** Warmth → wrongness → dread of the answer.

**Best case for it:** This is the highest raw hook intensity of any option and the most immediately marketable first frame. "The person beside you isn't there when you hold" is a self-explaining store preview.

**Stress test:**
- **It reinstates the rejected question.** "Is she real / who is she" is the external-mystery framing ("who is missing") that the v1.0 reframe explicitly killed in favor of "what did I do." Implication arrives later and weaker — the player investigates *her* instead of themselves.
- **Attribution gate sabotage.** A person disappearing under the player's Hold in minute one reads as haunting — the exact misattribution the Beat 5 gate exists to prevent. This challenger makes the hardest playtest gate harder.
- **It spends the game's most valuable withheld asset.** Canon presents the beloved through stillness, photo, and audio, with Her House (her chapter) as the meeting point. Presence in minute one devalues that entire arc and violates "mystery lives longer than comfort" in the one place the design deliberately holds mystery back.
- **Feasibility — worst of all options.** Even a silhouette-at-the-stove is staging, animation, and uncanny-valley risk spent in the single highest-stakes minute of the product. A badly staged figure in minute one can destroy tone irrecoverably on mobile hardware. Chapter 1 currently requires zero character presentation; that is a strength, not a gap.
- **Erasure engine lost.** Like Challenger 1, this has no natural seat for preference-drives-corruption; the opening becomes about her, and the baseline's causal loop is replaced by a reveal.

**Verdict on Challenger 2:** Wins narrowly on raw commercial hook intensity; loses decisively on implication, canon coherence, feasibility, and the attribution gate. The hook advantage is also partially redundant — the signature-transformation-per-chapter mandate already guarantees Chapter 1 a marketable clip without spending her. Rejected.

---

### Challenger 3 — "The Ritual" (enacted complicity; the player builds the lie)

**Philosophy:** Complicity through construction rather than preference. The player performs the delusion with their own hands before seeing it.

**Concept:** Before any Hold is taught, the player performs the morning: pour two coffees, set two places, turn the second chair toward the window she liked. Warm, tactile, interaction-dense (serving the density targets). Then the first Hold reveals that every action just performed was performed alone — the second mug already in the sink, unwashed for weeks; the chair's floor-wear showing it has not moved in months. "What did I do" becomes literal: *you did it, thirty seconds ago, on screen.*

**Emotional journey:** Domestic comfort → the floor dropping out of one's own actions → self-suspicion.

**Best case for it:** This is the only challenger that genuinely competes with the baseline on implication depth, and it arrives at implication through player action rather than player observation. It is also cheap: same diorama, no characters, interactions the House needs anyway.

**Stress test:**
- **The trick problem.** The revelation risks reading as "the game tricked me" — an external deception — rather than self-recognition. The baseline's implication emerges from the player's *voluntary, repeated* preference; the Ritual's implication is a one-time reveal staged by the designer. One is behavior; the other is a twist. Twists are consumed once; the baseline's loop compounds for the whole game.
- **Verb-grammar collision.** "Set the table" is P1 — The Set Table — WILT's first reconciliation puzzle, where the reconcile verb means *restoring* truth. Opening the game with the same object in inverted grammar (constructing the wish) risks contaminating the player's model of the core verb minutes before it is formally taught.
- **The erasure engine has no seat.** Preference-drives-corruption — the baseline's deepest element — either gets dropped (a major net loss) or bolted on after the ritual (sequencing bloat). The Ritual replaces a persistent behavioral engine with a single authored moment.
- **Pacing.** Sixty to ninety seconds of interactions before any hook, versus the baseline's hook arriving inside minute three *with* implication attached. On mobile, pre-hook chores are attrition.
- **Walkthrough test risk.** Unless every ritual action is emotionally authored, the sequence reads as tutorial chores — mechanisms, not realizations.

**Verdict on Challenger 3:** The strongest challenger. Genuinely different philosophy, real implication depth, low cost. But it beats the baseline on none of the five criteria decisively, and it forfeits the erasure engine — trading a compounding behavioral loop for a one-shot reveal. Rejected as a replacement. **One element is worth harvesting (§4).**

## 3. Collision analysis and verdict

| Criterion | Baseline v1.0 | C1 Release | C2 Guest | C3 Ritual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emotional impact | **High, compounding** (behavioral complicity loop) | High ceiling, canon-breaking | Front-loaded, then shallower | High, but one-shot |
| Player curiosity | **High** (self-directed: "what am I avoiding") | Medium | High but externally directed | Medium-high |
| Wished/True clarity | **High** (Wished-first, ambiguity preserved) | Damaged (True anchored as real) | Medium | High |
| Production feasibility | **Lowest cost** (2 nodes, 1 component) | Low cost, high tutorial risk | Worst (character staging) | Medium (authored interactions) |
| Commercial appeal | **High** (warm-hook artifact designed) | Weakened (cold open) | Highest raw hook, unacceptable cost | Medium |

**The decisive asymmetry:** every challenger replaces the baseline's implication engine with a weaker one. The baseline's unique, irreplaceable asset is the causal loop — *resting in comfort is itself the corrupting act.* Challenger 1 substitutes somatic relief (a sensation), Challenger 2 substitutes an external mystery (a question about someone else), Challenger 3 substitutes a retroactive reveal (a twist). Only the baseline turns the player's *ongoing, voluntary behavior* into the story's engine — and it is the only structure in which the Hold is the narrative itself rather than a viewing device pointed at the narrative.

**VERDICT: The locked Chapter 1 v1.0 opening remains the strongest available design. No challenger is superior; no challenger is equal.**

This conclusion was reached under genuinely adversarial construction — each challenger was built at its best case before being stress-tested — and it is falsifiable (§5). Confidence: **~85%**, capped below higher confidence only because the baseline's superiority rests on two empirical bets that have not yet been tested on players (the Beat 5 attribution gate and Hold ergonomics).

## 4. Harvested items (escalation candidates — NOT canon changes)

The exercise produced three by-products. None modifies v1.0; each is submitted for explicit accept/defer.

**H1 — Micro-enactment before Beat 2** *(from Challenger 3).* A single wordless maintenance interaction before the Wishwood pickup — e.g., the player straightens the second place setting, or re-hangs a slightly askew photo. One tap, no new systems, no dialogue. Purpose: pre-seed the "my hands maintain this" reading, so that when Beat 5's erasure fires, the player already has one memory of *actively tending* the wish. This should measurably strengthen Beat 5 attribution ("I've been maintaining this, and now my looking is breaking it"). Cost: one interaction, zero assets beyond existing props. Risk: adds ~10 seconds before the hook; must not read as a chore. **Recommendation: accept as a candidate for the Beat 5 playtest A/B — test the opening with and without it. Defer any lock until the gate runs.**

**H2 — New instrumentation for the Hold ergonomics spike** *(from Challenger 1).* Challenger 1's failure exposed a real threat to the baseline: **thumb fatigue can counterfeit comfort-seeking.** If players release the Hold because their hand is tired rather than because they crave Wished, then (a) the Return test in the Visual Direction validation set gives a false positive, and (b) Beat 5's erasure fires on a physical accident, poisoning attribution. The ergonomics spike should therefore explicitly separate the two motives: vary required hold durations across test cells, and add exit-interview language distinguishing "I wanted the warm version back" from "my hand needed a break." If fatigue dominates release behavior at realistic durations, that is a design-level problem for the erasure rule, not just a UX problem. **Recommendation: accept — this is a cheap addition to a spike already planned, and it protects the design's most load-bearing gate.**

**H3 — Confirmation, not change** *(from Challenger 2).* Her absence from Chapter 1 is a validated strength. The challenger built specifically to exploit her presence lost on four of five criteria. No action; recorded so the question does not silently reopen later.

---

## 5. What would falsify this verdict

The verdict stands unless one of the following occurs. If any does, this exercise should be re-run with the new evidence, because the baseline's superiority depends on these bets:

1. **The Beat 5 attribution gate fails twice** — including once after legibility fixes. If players persistently read the erasure as glitch or haunting, the baseline loses its engine, and Challenger 3's construction-based implication becomes the leading alternative (it is the only challenger whose implication does not depend on the erasure rule).
2. **The Hold ergonomics spike shows fatigue-dominant release** at durations the design needs. If truth-holding is physically unsustainable, "denial as resting state" degrades from a theme into an annoyance, and the entire preference-drives-corruption structure must be re-examined.
3. **The first-sixty-seconds artifact underperforms** in store-preview testing with the warm open. This would reopen the hook-intensity question, where Challenger 2's core (not its execution) holds the only unexploited advantage.

Open items carried forward unchanged from v1.0: Beat 4 (person-shaped gap) and Beats 0–1 (visual emphasis) remain deferred, untouched by this review.
